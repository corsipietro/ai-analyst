### Explanation via Model Equations
The debris population $N$ evolves according to:
$$\Delta N = f \cdot c - \frac{N}{\tau}$$
where $c$ (collisions) is approximately $\alpha \cdot S \cdot N$. For the debris population to be stable ($\Delta N = 0$), the generation rate must equal the decay rate:
$$f \cdot (\alpha \cdot S \cdot N) = \frac{N}{\tau} \implies 1 = f \cdot \alpha \cdot S \cdot \tau$$

Using the provided parameters:
*   $f = 100$
*   $\alpha = 10^{-6}$
*   $\tau = 25$

We can solve for the **critical satellite population ($S_{crit}$)**:
$$S_{crit} = \frac{1}{f \cdot \alpha \cdot \tau} = \frac{1}{100 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 25} = \frac{1}{0.0025} = 400 \text{ satellites}$$

*   If $S > 400$, debris grows because collisions create more fragments than drag can remove.
*   If $S < 400$, debris decays because the removal rate exceeds the creation rate.

The satellite population $S$ is driven by the launch rate $L$ and the average life $T=5$. In a steady state (ignoring the small impact of collisions on $S$ for this estimation), $S \approx L \cdot T$. Therefore, the **critical launch rate ($L_{crit}$)** is:
$$L_{crit} \approx \frac{S_{crit}}{T} = \frac{400}{5} = 80 \text{ launches/year}$$

This explains why $L=40$ and $L=70$ lead to debris reduction, while $L=100$ and $L=120$ lead to rapid debris accumulation. At $L=80$, the system is at the tipping point.
