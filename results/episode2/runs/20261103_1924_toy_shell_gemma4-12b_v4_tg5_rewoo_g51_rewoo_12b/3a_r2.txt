### Theoretical Explanation
The behavior can be explained by comparing the **creation rate** and the **removal rate** from the model equations:
1. **Creation Rate:** $f \cdot \alpha \cdot S \cdot N$ (where $f=100$ and $\alpha=10^{-6}$)
2. **Removal Rate:** $N / \tau$ (where $\tau=25$)

A steady state is reached when these two rates are equal. Dividing both sides by $N$, we can solve for the critical satellite population ($S_{crit}$) required to maintain a constant debris level:
$$f \cdot \alpha \cdot S = 1 / \tau$$
$$S_{crit} = \frac{1 / \tau}{f \cdot \alpha} = \frac{1 / 25}{100 \cdot 10^{-6}} = \frac{0.04}{0.0001} = 400 \text{ satellites [computed]}$$

**How the data matches this:**
- **Low Launch (L=40):** The satellite population stabilizes at approximately $S \approx 200$ [tool]. Since $200 < 400$, the removal rate ($1/25 = 0.04$) exceeds the creation rate ($0.02$), causing the debris to eventually decrease.
- **High Launch (L=120):** The satellite population stabilizes at approximately $S \approx 590$ [tool]. Since $590 > 400$, the creation rate ($\approx 0.059$) exceeds the removal rate ($0.04$), leading to the continuous growth observed in the data.

### Limitations
- This analysis is based on a simplified model where collisions are the only source of debris and atmospheric drag is the only removal mechanism. 
- The "peak" observed in some lower-launch runs may be a result of the system transitioning from the initial state ($S=1000, N=1000$) toward its new equilibrium.
- Further data on the rate of change of $S$ would be needed to confirm exactly when the system reaches equilibrium for each $L$.
