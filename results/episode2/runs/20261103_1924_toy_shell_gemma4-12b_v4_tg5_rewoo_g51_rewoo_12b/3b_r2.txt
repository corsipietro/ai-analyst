### Physical Explanation
The behavior of the debris population is governed by the equation:
$N(t+1) = \max(0, N + f \cdot c - N/\tau)$ [context]

The dynamics depend on the balance between:
1.  **Production ($f \cdot c$):** The number of new debris objects created by collisions. Since $c \propto S \cdot N$ [context], the production rate is directly proportional to the number of active satellites ($S$) and the current amount of debris ($N$).
2.  **Removal ($-N/\tau$):** The rate at which debris is removed by atmospheric drag ($\tau = 25$ years).

The launch rate $L$ determines the equilibrium population of active satellites $S$. 
*   When **$L$ is low**, the satellite population $S$ remains small enough that the collision frequency $c$ is low. The removal term $N/\tau$ eventually outweighs the production $f \cdot c$, causing the debris to fall out of the shell.
*   When **$L$ is high**, the satellite population $S$ is large enough that the collision frequency $c$ becomes very high. The production $f \cdot c$ exceeds the removal $N/\tau$, leading to a runaway growth of debris because each new collision creates 100 fragments ($f=100$) [context].

**Limits of this conclusion:** This analysis is based on a toy model with a fixed $\alpha=10^{-6}$ and $f=100$. In a real system, $\alpha$ would vary with orbital parameters, and the growth might be even more non-linear. The data shows 3 repetitions per run, which is a small sample size; while the trends are clear, the high variance (spread) at $L=120$ suggests significant stochasticity in the collision counts.
