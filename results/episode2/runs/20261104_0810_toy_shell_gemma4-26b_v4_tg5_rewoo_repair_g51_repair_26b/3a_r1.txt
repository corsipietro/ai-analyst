*   **Transition Zone ($L=80$ to $90$):** Debris shows significant growth and peaks, but for $L=90$, it begins to decline after year 21 (peak ~1752) as the satellite population is suppressed by collisions [tool].
*   **High Launches ($L \ge 100$):** Debris grows rapidly and continuously. At $L=120$, debris reaches a mean of ~2827 by year 60 [tool].

### Mathematical Explanation
The behavior is driven by the balance between debris creation (collisions) and debris removal (drag). The rate of change for debris is:
$$\Delta N \approx (f \cdot c) - \frac{N}{\tau}$$
Substituting the collision equation $c \approx \alpha \cdot S \cdot N$:
$$\Delta N \approx N \left( f \cdot \alpha \cdot S - \frac{1}{\tau} \right)$$

The "tipping point" occurs when the term in the parentheses is zero. This defines a critical number of satellites ($S_{crit}$) required to sustain debris growth:
$$S_{crit} = \frac{1}{f \cdot \alpha \cdot \tau}$$

Using the provided parameters:
$S_{crit} = \frac{1}{100 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 25} = 400$ [computed]

*   **If $S < 400$:** The debris removal rate ($N/\tau$) exceeds the creation rate ($f \cdot c$), and $N$ decreases. This happens when $L$ is low (e.g., $L=40 \implies S \approx 200$).
*   **If $S > 400$:** The creation rate exceeds the removal rate, and $N$ grows. This happens at high launch rates (e.g., $L=120 \implies S \approx 600$).
*   **The "Peak" Effect:** In intermediate cases, $N$ grows and increases the collision rate $c$. This higher collision rate reduces the number of active satellites ($S$) in the next step. If $S$ is pushed below 400, the debris growth reverses and $N$ starts to fall.

**Limits:** These conclusions are based on the provided toy model and its specific parameters. In a real system, the relationship between $S$ and $N$ is far more complex, and the threshold for "Kessler Syndrome" depends on many more orbital variables.
