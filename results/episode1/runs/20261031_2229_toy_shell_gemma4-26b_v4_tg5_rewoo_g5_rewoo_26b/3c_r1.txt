For example, at $L=40$, the satellite count settles around $199$ [tool], and the debris population drops from its initial $1000$ to approximately $570$ by year 60 [tool].
*   **High Launch Rates ($L \ge 90$):** The satellite population stabilizes above the critical threshold. This triggers a feedback loop where increased collisions create more debris, which in turn increases the collision rate. At $L=120$, the satellite population reaches $\sim 591$ [tool], causing the debris population to explode from $1000$ to over $2827$ [tool].

### Model Explanation
The behavior can be explained by the debris evolution equation:
$$\Delta N \approx f \cdot c - \frac{N}{\tau}$$
where the collision rate $c \approx \alpha \cdot S \cdot N$. Substituting $c$, the rate of change for debris is:
$$\frac{dN}{dt} \approx (f \cdot \alpha \cdot S \cdot \tau - 1) \frac{N}{\tau}$$

A critical threshold exists when the rate of debris generation ($f \cdot \alpha \cdot S \cdot N$) equals the rate of debris removal ($N/\tau$). We can solve for the critical number of satellites ($S_{crit}$):
$$S_{crit} = \frac{1}{f \cdot \alpha \cdot \tau}$$

Using the provided parameters:
*   $\alpha = 10^{-6}$
*   $f = 100$
*   $\tau = 25$

$$S_{crit} = \frac{1}{100 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 25} = \frac{1}{0.0025} = 400 \text{ [computed]}$$

**Conclusion:** 
When the launch rate $L$ is high enough to sustain a satellite population $S > 400$, the debris population grows uncontrollably because the production from collisions outpaces natural decay. If $S < 400$, the orbital environment is self-cleaning as the atmospheric drag removes debris faster than collisions create it.
