### Theoretical Explanation
The behavior is governed by the interaction of the following model equations [context]:
1.  **Satellite Growth:** $S(t+1) = S + L - S/T - c$. The launch rate $L$ directly determines the population of active satellites $S$.
2.  **Collision Rate:** $c \sim \text{Poisson}(\alpha \cdot S \cdot N)$. The number of collisions $c$ is proportional to the product of the number of satellites and the amount of debris.
3.  **Debris Dynamics:** $N(t+1) = N + f \cdot c - N/\tau$. The debris population grows if the production from collisions ($f \cdot c$) exceeds the natural decay due to atmospheric drag ($N/\tau$).

**Conclusion:**
At lower launch rates ($L < 90$), the system reaches a state where the decay $N/\tau$ (where $\tau = 25$ years) balances the collision production. Because $S$ remains relatively low, the collision rate $c$ stays small enough that the decay removes debris faster than it is created. 

At higher launch rates ($L \ge 90$), the increased number of satellites $S$ drives a higher collision rate $c$. Because the number of fragments per collision is large ($f=100$), this creates a positive feedback loop: more debris leads to more collisions, which generates even more debris, eventually overwhelming the decay rate $N/\tau$.

**Limitations:**
These results are specific to the current model parameters (e.g., $\alpha = 10^{-6}$, $f = 100$). If the collision coefficient $\alpha$ were significantly lower or the fragments per collision $f$ were smaller, the "breakout" point where debris growth becomes runaway would occur at a higher launch rate.
